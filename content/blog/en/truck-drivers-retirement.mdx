## Quick summary

- Independent owner-operators are self-employed, so employer-sponsored plans are not available to them.
- An Indexed Universal Life policy is not an employer plan and does not end when a contract does, which is the structural reason it comes up in this context.
- Its premiums are adjustable, which fits income that varies by season, freight rate and fuel cost.
- The same flexibility carries a risk: a policy that is not funded enough to carry its own charges can lapse, and the death benefit goes with it.

Retirement planning advice generally assumes a regular paycheck, an employer plan and a matching contribution. An owner-operator has none of those. This article covers what that changes and what it does not.

## Why the usual advice does not fit

Income in this business varies — by season, by freight rate, by fuel cost, by how many loads run in a month. There will be good months and months where costs take most of the revenue.

Most contribution-based plans assume a steady deduction from a regular paycheck. That is the mismatch: the plan expects consistency the work does not produce.

## What an IUL does differently

Two structural things, neither of which is a recommendation to buy one:

**It is not an employer plan.** It does not depend on anyone offering it, it is not tied to a carrier you drive for or a state you are registered in, and it does not end when a contract does. If you change who you haul for, the policy is unaffected.

**Its premiums are adjustable.** You can pay more in a strong month and less in a slow one, within the limits the contract sets.

## The risk that comes with that flexibility

This is the part that is usually sold as a benefit, and it deserves to be read the other way round.

A policy stays in force while there is enough cash value to cover its monthly charges. That is what allows you to pay less — or nothing — for a period without the cover ending.

It is also how these policies fail. **If the cash value is drawn down and the charges are not covered, the policy lapses — and the death benefit lapses with it.** A season of low payments is a feature. Several of them, early on when the cash value is still small, is how people lose cover they have paid into for years.

If you take one thing from this article: ask what the minimum funding level is to keep the policy in force, and what happens if you fall below it.

## How the cash value works

Interest is credited to the cash value according to the movement of a market index. Your money is not in the market and you do not hold the index.

If the index falls, the crediting rate does not go negative and the index loss is not credited against the value you have already built. The cap works the other way — it is the most that can be credited in a period, however far the index rose. The policy also has its own internal charges, and those do not stop in a period when crediting does.

## Using the cash value on the road

Cash value can be accessed through policy loans, which is why drivers ask about it for equipment or repairs.

Two things belong next to that. A loan **accrues interest**. And a loan still outstanding when the policy pays is **settled out of the death benefit**, so what reaches your family is reduced by what was borrowed and by the interest on it. Borrowing against a policy is not the same as taking money out of a savings account, and it should not be planned as though it were.

## Applying with an ITIN

Many independent drivers file with an ITIN. Carriers exist that accept an ITIN in place of a Social Security Number on a life insurance application, and which ones do — and what else they ask for — varies between them.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I get a policy with variable income?** Yes. Adjustable premiums are a normal feature of these contracts. What varies is the minimum funding needed to keep the policy in force, which is set by the contract and by how it was structured at the outset.

**What happens if I take time off the road?** The policy continues while the cash value covers its monthly charges. Once it does not, the policy is at risk. How long that period lasts depends on how much value has accumulated, which is why the early years matter most.

**Do I need a Social Security Number?** Not necessarily. Some carriers accept an ITIN. See the article on ITIN applications for what the process involves.

**Can I borrow against it to buy a truck?** A policy loan can generally be taken for any purpose. Read the section above on what a loan costs before treating it as available cash.

**What should I ask before signing?** The minimum funding level, what happens if you fall below it, the charge structure in the early years, the current cap, and what the contract guarantees as opposed to what the illustration projects.
